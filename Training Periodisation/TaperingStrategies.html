The Banister Impulse-Response Model

This section visualizes the theoretical foundation of tapering. The Banister model posits that athletic performance is the difference between acquired Fitness (slow to build, slow to decay) and accumulated Fatigue (fast to build, fast to decay). Interact with the chart below to see why power athletes require drastically different tapers than endurance athletes.

Endurance Profile

Endurance athletes accumulate deep, systemic metabolic fatigue. However, their cardiovascular fitness decays very slowly. Therefore, they benefit from a longer taper (2-3 weeks). The extended time allows massive fatigue to dissipate fully while aerobic capacity remains near peak levels, creating a massive positive spike in Net Performance.

Power Profile

Power athletes generate immense Central Nervous System (CNS) fatigue. CNS fatigue dissipates rapidly (within days). Crucially, neuromuscular activation and "explosive fitness" also decay quickly. A long taper would result in detraining. Thus, they require a short, sharp taper (1 week) to drop CNS fatigue while keeping the neuromuscular system highly stimulated.

Step vs. Exponential Tapering

How you reduce training volume matters. Explore the structural differences between immediate drops (Step) and gradual reductions (Exponential).

Less Common

Step Taper

An immediate, abrupt reduction in training load (e.g., dropping volume by 50% on day one and holding it there). Often leads to feelings of sluggishness in endurance athletes, but can be useful for power athletes needing an immediate CNS reset.

Exponential Taper

A progressive, mathematical reduction in volume. Can be 'Fast Decay' (drops quickly early on) or 'Slow Decay' (drops smoothly). Scientifically proven to yield the highest performance gains (average 2-3% improvement) across most sports.
