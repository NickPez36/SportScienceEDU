Neurophysiological & Biomechanical Mechanisms

This section deconstructs the underlying physiology of RSI, focusing on the Stretch-Shortening Cycle (SSC). Understanding the interaction between neural reflexes and mechanical tissue properties is critical for analyzing reactive strength. Interact with the elements below to explore the distinct mechanisms.

The Stretch-Shortening Cycle (SSC)

  • ▸ Muscle Spindle Reflexes: Detect rate and magnitude of stretch. Rapid lengthening triggers an excitatory Ia afferent signal, causing a powerful reflexive contraction (myotatic reflex) that augments concentric force.
  • ▸ Golgi Tendon Organs (GTOs): Act as a protective mechanism detecting tension. High forces trigger an inhibitory Ib signal. Elite training downregulates GTO sensitivity, allowing greater force expression during the SSC.
  • ▸ Fascicle Behavior: During efficient SSC, muscle fascicles remain relatively isometric (quasi-isometric) while the tendinous tissues lengthen, optimizing the length-tension relationship.
  • ▸ Series Elastic Component (SEC): Tendons and aponeuroses act like springs, storing elastic strain energy during the eccentric phase and returning it during the concentric phase, amplifying power output without additional metabolic cost.

Fast vs. Slow SSC Thresholds

The classification of SSC is strictly dictated by Ground Contact Time (GCT).

Characterized by short ground contact times. Relies heavily on tendon stiffness, short-latency stretch reflexes, and elastic energy return. Foundational for maximum velocity sprinting and rapid change of direction.

Measurement and Quantification

Accurate quantification of RSI dictates programming efficacy. This section outlines the gold-standard testing protocols, foundational equations, and hardware validity required in elite sports science settings.

Foundational Formulas

Standard RSI (Drop Jumps)

RSI = Jump Height (m) / Ground Contact Time (s)

Used primarily in single-effort assessments from an external load (drop height) to measure rapid force absorption and expression.

Modified RSI (Repeated Jumps)

RSImod = Flight Time (s) / Ground Contact Time (s)

Utilized in cyclical tests (e.g., 10/5 Repeated Jump Test) where true jump height is difficult to ascertain continuously without force plates. Flight time acts as a surrogate for height.

Testing Protocols

  • Incremental Drop Jump Profile Athlete drops from increasing box heights (e.g., 15, 30, 45, 60cm). Identifies the specific height at which RSI peaks before degrading (due to GCT increasing or height dropping). Establishes individual optimal dropping height.
  • 10/5 Repeated Jump Test Athlete performs one maximal countermovement jump followed by 10 continuous, maximal repeated bounds with minimal ground contact. Averages the best 5. Excellent for cyclical power assessment.

Hardware Considerations in Elite Settings

Hardware Type Mechanism Validity/Reliability Application Context
Force Plates (Uni/Tri-axial) Measures vertical ground reaction forces at >1000Hz. Derives data via impulse-momentum. Gold Standard Research, elite laboratory settings. Calculates true jump height from takeoff velocity.
Contact Mats Circuit closes upon pressure. Calculates flight time. Moderate-High Field testing. Can overestimate jump height if athlete tucks knees during flight.
Optical Systems (Lasers/Cameras) Beam breaking or high-speed kinematic tracking. High (if sampling >120Hz) Excellent field alternative to force plates. Non-invasive.

Training Applications and Periodization

Applying RSI data involves structured plyometric progressions and rigorous daily monitoring. Understanding the dose-response relationship of drop heights is paramount to avoiding central nervous system (CNS) overtraining and structural damage.

Individualizing Optimal Drop Height

Depth jumps place extreme eccentric loads on the neuromuscular system. By testing RSI across increasing box heights, practitioners identify the "Peak RSI". Training above this height results in prolonged GCT, indicating the athlete cannot handle the eccentric braking forces, thereby shifting the adaptation away from fast SSC.

Plyometric Progression

  • 1. Extensive (Force Absorption):
    Low amplitude, high volume. Focus on landing mechanics and stiffness. e.g., Pogo jumps, low box jumps.
  • 2. Intensive (Force Expression):
    Moderate amplitude, moderate volume. Focus on minimizing GCT. e.g., Hurdle hops, bounds.
  • 3. Shock Method (Maximal SSC):
    High amplitude, low volume. Utilizing kinetic energy from a drop. e.g., Depth jumps from optimized height.

RSI as a Readiness Monitor

RSI (often via a standard Drop Jump or 10/5 test) is highly sensitive to neuromuscular fatigue. It is widely used in daily monitoring routines prior to training sessions.

Actionable Data: A drop in baseline RSI > 10% indicates acute CNS fatigue or delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS). Programming should be adjusted to remove high-velocity/high-load stressors for that microcycle.
